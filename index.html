Understanding User Requirements

Now that we know the process of designing and engineering a new product. Let's look at the first step: how we capture the User Needs.

The Challenges in Capturing User Needs

Before we write the user requirements, we must look at the System Context. Using our vending machine example, think about how it is actually used.

💬

Consider your reaction after receiving a client brief

Results

Imagine your team just received this official project brief from a client:
"We need a modern, user-friendly snack and drink machine installed in the campus building. It should be fast, reliable, convenient, and look sleek."

When you receive a brief like this, how do you want to start engineering?

A. Accept the brief as is and start designing the machine immediately. 0%
B. Carry out a study based on a previous vending machine project to save time. 0%
C. Ask the client clarifying questions to understand their context, environment, and user base. 0%
D. Push the brief back to the client and demand a technical specification. 0%
Observing physical posture, reach, payment, and retrieval ergonomics
Capturing a user's "needs" is rarely straightforward.

Think of a user requirement as a wish list:

  • Users are not professionals who know exactly what they are after.
  • It is unreasonable to expect the user to provide an exact list of requirements or to adhere to it throughout the system's development.
  • The "requirements problem" always stems from the difficulty in identifying, interpreting, and understanding the most fundamental hopes of the user.

How to Capture User Needs

For the vending machine system, you might have used it before. Imagine yourself as a user of it.

🔍

Think about how you interact with the system and what you need from it.

Consider which buttons the user has to press, how they pay (cash, coin or make a digital purchase), how they pick up the product, whether they need a straw, what to do if the user needs to call for help/report, and whether to take change.

Writing Measurable User Requirements

Finally, let's turn a "wish" into a "requirement". A good requirement must be Specific, Measurable, and Verifiable. It is very common to find this challenging when writing user requirements.

✏️

Test your skills in identifying measurable requirements.

Written Requirements Is it a measurable requirement? Why?
It should accept many payments.
The system shall dispense the selected product within 5 seconds of payment confirmation.
The system shall support Cash, Visa, Mastercard, and Momo QR payments.
The machine should be fast.
The system shall maintain internal storage temperature between 2°C and 5°C.
The drinks should be cold.

Good work! In class, we will collaborate on a client brief to practise identifying user needs for a complex engineering project. You will write the user requirements, which is a core part of your upcoming assessment.

Option 1: Colour Theory in Digital Design

Colour Theory in Digital Design

Context: You are currently working in design teams to create a mobile app for campus students. Before finalising your high-fidelity user interface, work through this guide to understand how colour choices directly impact user experience and navigation.

Why does colour matter?

Have you ever visited a website and immediately felt overwhelmed, or struggled to read light-grey text on a white background? Colour isn’t just decoration—it quietly guides how people feel, where they look, and whether an app feels intuitive or exhausting to use.

The Colour Wheel: Harmony & Contrast

You don’t need an art degree to build a great palette. Most everyday choices come down to two basic ideas:

Complementary Colours: These sit directly opposite each other on the colour wheel (like deep blue and bright orange). Because they clash in high contrast, they naturally grab attention. They’re fantastic for warning badges or a big "Submit" button you want no one to miss.

Analogous Colours: These sit right next to each other (like sky blue, teal, and soft green). They feel calm, cohesive, and easy on the eyes. They make great backgrounds because they don’t fight for attention.

The Catch: Contrast and Legibility

A combination might look aesthetic to you, but if a student in bright sunlight or someone with colour vision differences can’t read it, the design fails. Good design always balances mood with readable contrast ratios.

(End of text. In the final assessment, students have to design a complete, accessible app landing page.)

Event Budget Planning

Context: Your committee is organising a commercial campus event for 300 attendees. Before pitching your financial plan to the sponsors, review this foundational guide on managing fixed versus variable operational expenses.

Where does all the money go?

Organising an event is exciting until the bills arrive. The number-one reason campus festivals, charity fundraisers, or conferences run into trouble isn’t a lack of great ideas—it’s surprise costs that spiral out of control.

Two Buckets: Fixed vs. Variable Costs

To keep sanity and finances intact, event planners split expenses into two straightforward buckets:

Fixed Costs (The Non-Negotiables): These stay the exact same whether 10 people show up or 200 do. Think venue hire, stage permits, standard insurance, or sound equipment. You pay for them once.

Variable Costs (The Scale-Ups): These grow with every person who walks through the door. Think catering portions, printed name badges, welcome drinks, and extra security staff.

The Golden Rule: The Contingency Buffer

Even the best-laid plans go sideways. A caterer might cancel last minute, or rain forces you to hire an emergency marquee. That’s why planners always set aside a 10% to 15% rainy-day buffer. If you don’t plan for surprises upfront, they come out of your own pocket.

(End of text. For their main project, students must submit a viable commercial budget for a 300-person event.)

Three-Point Studio Lighting

Context: As part of your upcoming digital video assessment, you must light and record an interview subject in the broadcast studio. Learn how to transform flat camera imagery into three-dimensional cinematic depth.

Why do smartphone videos look flat?

Have you ever tried recording an interview or a presentation video on your phone, only to look washed out, tired, or lost in the background? Real life is three-dimensional, but cameras flatten everything onto a 2D screen. Lighting is the trick that puts life, shape, and depth back in.

The Classic Three-Light Recipe

You don’t need a Hollywood budget to get a cinematic look. Most professional shoots rely on three basic lights:

Key Light: Your main hero light. It sits off to one side at an angle, giving the face shape and character while casting natural shadows.

Fill Light: Placed on the other side. Its job is gentle teamwork—it softens those dark shadows so the subject doesn’t look harsh or dramatic (unless that’s what you want!).

Back Light (Hair Light): Placed behind and above, pointing down. It paints a subtle halo of light along the shoulders and hair, popping the person forward so they don’t melt into a dark wall.

The Catch: Mood and Balance

Adjusting just one dimmer switch completely changes the story—from a friendly YouTube explainer to a moody film-noir interrogation.

(End of text. In their studio assessment, students must light a live actor to match a specific emotional scene.)
