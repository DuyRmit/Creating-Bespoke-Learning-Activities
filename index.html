How to Capture User Needs

Context:

You are a business analyst. The RMIT campus executive just gave you a project on designing the campus vending machine. They said that students want a good, fast, convenient one.

So in order for you to communicate to your design and engineering team, you'll need to put yourself into the role of the user and identify what's needed.

Capturing User Needs

🔍

Think about how you interact with the system and what you need from it.

Consider which buttons the user has to press, how they pay (cash, coin, or make a digital purchase), how they pick up the product, whether they need a straw, what to do if the user needs to call for help/report, and whether to take change.

1. Test the machine

Click through the SmartDrinks simulation below to complete a purchase. Notice how the keypad, screen, payment options, and change tray work in practice.

2. Identify friction

Pay attention to what felt smooth versus what felt confusing or missing while trying to get your item.

🎯 3. Suggested Testing Scenarios
Scenario A: Cash Purchase

Try to buy a Pepsi (A2) using cash banknotes.

Scenario B: Instant QR Payment

Select La Vie Water (B1) and pay instantly with the QR scanner.

Scenario C: Overpayment & Change

Insert 50,000đ for a 7-Up (A3) and collect your change from the tray.

After experiencing the vending machine, you probably have some ideas on what you want the machine to do based on your simulation experience. Now that you have a brief idea and understanding of the user experience, let's put it into words.

📝

Capture your thoughts:

Write down a quick, informal wishlist of features or fixes based on what you experienced.

Example Wishlist:
"I want to be able to pay with card." "I want the buttons to be bigger." "I want to return the drinks / cancel an order."

💬 Wishlist Discussion 0 notes

Share your informal wishlist notes with your peers and discuss common friction moments.

Writing Measurable User Requirements

After noting down your observation, you bring those to discuss with your design and engineering team. However, they said that your wishlist is not clear enough. The team can't design if the requirements are not specific, measurable, and verifiable. What does that mean?

Specific

The requirement details exactly what needs to happen and what the system should do.

Measurable

The requirement includes concrete numbers, limits, or performance metrics.

Verifiable

There must be a clear way to test and confirm the feature works.

It is very common to find this challenging when writing user requirements. People often stop at vague wishes instead of actionable criteria.

✏️

Practise identifying good requirements that are Specific, Measurable, and Verifiable.

Written Requirements Is it a measurable requirement? Why?
It should accept many payments.
The system shall dispense the selected product within 5 seconds of payment confirmation.
The system shall support Cash, Visa, Mastercard, and Momo QR payments.
The machine should be fast.
The system shall maintain internal storage temperature between 2°C and 5°C.
The drinks should be cold.
🔄

Revisit your own wishlist

Let's revisit your own wishlist in the previous activity. How would you rewrite them into requirements that are Specific, Measurable, and Verifiable?

Good work!

Hope you have a better understanding how to capture user needs and transform them into Specific, Measurable, and Verifiable requirements.

Colour Theory in Digital Design

Colour Theory in Digital Design

Context:

You're working in design teams to create a mobile app for campus students. Before finalising your high-fidelity interface, work through this guide to understand how colour choices directly affect readability and navigation.

Let's explore how we can evaluate whether a colour combination meets basic contrast standards, and explain why that matters for real users.

1. Why contrast matters

Have you ever struggled to read an app outside in bright sunlight? That's a contrast problem, and it affects almost everyone at some point, not just people with visual impairments.

Contrast is simply how different two colours look next to each other. Dark text on a white background has high contrast. Light grey text on white has low contrast. The higher the contrast, the easier something is to read.

2. Why it's a bigger deal than it sounds

When designing with colour, it's easy to focus on mood and vibe and forget function. Picture reading your phone in bright sunlight, using an app late at night with tired eyes, or being colourblind and unable to tell a red warning from a green confirmation. Good contrast solves all of these at once, it's what makes an app usable in real conditions, not just on a designer's monitor.

Apps that get this right share a few habits:

  • Dark text on light backgrounds, or light text on dark backgrounds
  • Buttons that clearly stand out from the page around them
  • Warnings or errors that don't rely on colour alone to make their point
3. Fix the palette
⭐ Prime Simulation Opportunity

Imagine you've been given a mobile app screen from a campus events app. The current design uses:

  • Header: Light grey text (#B0B0B0) on a white background (#FFFFFF)
  • Primary button: Yellow (#F5E050) with white text (#FFFFFF)
  • Body text: Medium grey (#999999) on white (#FFFFFF)

All three combinations fail WCAG contrast standards. Your task:

  • Use a contrast checker (such as WebAIM's Contrast Checker) to test each of the three combinations above. Record the contrast ratio for each.
  • Adjust each colour pair until it passes: at least 4.5:1 for body text, and at least 3:1 for large text and UI components like buttons.
  • Re-check your new combinations and confirm they pass.
  • Write two to three sentences: what did you change, and why does it now work better for a user standing outside in bright sunlight?
4. Check your understanding of colour contrast

Inspect whether each pair meets readable standards (≥ 4.5:1):

Option A
Numbers Button
Black on White (#000000 on #FFFFFF)
Option B
Font Button
Light Blue on White (#A0C4FF on #FFFFFF)
Option C
Typography
White on Navy (#FFFFFF on #1A1A2E)

Good job! Hopefully you can take away at least one practice you'll carry into your own app design assessment.
